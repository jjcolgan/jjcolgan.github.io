Defining the Functional and Genomic Dynamics Governing Host-Microbe Mutualism Throughout the Intestinal Tract

The small and large intestine present heterogeneous environments to resident bacteria, imposing distinct selective pressures on microbial communities across regions. Using an RB-TnSeq mutant library based on Bifidobacterium breve, I defined the genomic features required for bacterial engraftment in both the small and large intestine. Complementary bulk RNA-seq profiling was used to elucidate the host transcriptional response regionally and temporally, enabling direct interrogation of the relationship between host gene expression and microbial mutant fitness.

This work uncovered novel insights into the bacterial functions governing region-specific engraftment, and characterized the distinct host responses to microbial colonization along the intestinal tract. Most notably, it revealed associations between mutant fitness and host gene expression — providing new evidence for how host and microbe cooperate to establish mutually beneficial systems.

This project represents my doctoral dissertation at the University of Chicago.

02

Microbial and Metabolomic Changes Following Ilealization of the Colon

Previous work in the Chang lab has demonstrated that mismatched intestinal microbiota — such as large intestinal bacteria invading the small intestine — can perturb regional host identity. Here, we asked the inverse question: whether perturbations of host regional identity can drive changes in the resident microbial community. To address this, we employed a conditional knockout of SATB2, a chromatin-associated factor critical for regulating colonocyte identity, which induces ilealization of the colonic epithelium and thereby alters the regional environment available to resident bacteria.

Using metagenomics and metabolomics, we identified novel associations between shifts in bacterial and metabolite abundances and changes in metagenomic functional potential following loss of host colonic identity. Together, these findings demonstrate that resident microbiome composition can be reshaped by changes to regional host identity — illustrating the bidirectional nature of host-microbe crosstalk.

This work was conducted at the University of Chicago and was led by Jingwen Xu, a senior graduate student in the Chang lab. As a part of this project, I performed the metagenomic and metabolomic analysis.
